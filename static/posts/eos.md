It's a really long story. Writing this because I'm quite proud of this entire project and getting this far :) - so thanks to everyone who's been even a small part of it.

To put the timeline in perspective, I'm 17 this year (2026) and I've had the aspiration of starting such a project since I think last year ~May/June -ish. However, work didn't really start until October-ish last year, and the team itself was only assembled around mid-February.

Last note, in my next post, I will be writing more in-depth about my experience and the more personal reasons I had for starting this project. Will be up sometime next week, so do come back if you're interested :)

## Solo-ing

At first, I had no idea how to start despite really wanting to. I knew that the gender ratio in computing in my school was truly horrendous (check out my other post later, where I talked more about the situation girls in computing in general), and as I became more social (shocking, I know), I found that the situation was not limited to my school. (Side note: Yep, the gender ratio in STEM is already rather unbalanced, but in computing and tech, it's a completely different story.) And so, yeah, I wanted to change the experience girls would have while exploring tech-related fields. But I had no one to work with because I didn't really know any girls interested in computing who would be interested -- the Y6s in my school were too busy, I'm the only one in my cohort, the Y4s non-existent (yeah, 0 CS majors) and I would prefer to work with Senior High and above.

In July, I applied and then joined a programme in my school meant to support students in working towards the PSC Scholarships, but I thought of it more as a developmental programme in terms of character, leadership, extra-curriculars and such. Essentially you were assigned a teacher-mentor and they were meant to give you advice for whatever it is you chose to do. There is no structure -- you were just encouraged to start a passion project (which in itself has no proper definition), and aside from occasional networking session, there wasn't much else. I wouldn't say I started my project because of this programme, but it was definitely another excuse to do it -- plus, now I had a teacher-mentor that could maybe lend me some opinions and provide some resources.

Then someone I met at a computing-related competition heard about my plan and hooked me up with a computing girl in his class and we starting discussing some plans (this was maybe August). After developing the idea slightly (deciding elements that would matter to me in this project, and certain details on execution), she decided not to continue working on this with me as she was busy and would like to focus on other stuff.

Then I made a plan and slides and design assets, a pitch deck and other whatnot. That took the whole of December because I also enjoy side-questing. 

## Assembling a team

In January, I asked absolutely random people in other JCs (all guys because I didn't know any other girls oop) to spread the word. Most of them heavily implied that ok, they will spread my message for me, but don't expect many/any responses. Also, at this point, from sheer desperation, I became kind of shameless (saying 'kind of' here, because it gets a lot worse later on) and asked the most random people in the most random discord servers to broadcast the message for me. I think at least 3 people told me they were impressed by how far my message went because I was asking around everywhere and anywhere I could. Looking back, no regrets on this part, shamelessness was definitely the way to go. A funny story was where someone decided to summarise my message and left out the 'girls' part of it and a bunch of guys reached out and I was so confused.

Now, a problem here was that I can only get J1s. J2 A levels, Sec 4, O levels. Now, what did my brain forget? That my school don't have this transition from secondary to JC, but everyone else does. The issue? I can't go through computing-related CCAs in JCs, since they wont join their JC until February, and CCA until April. So after going through chains to find people going from eg. NYGH Sec 4, moving to HCI J1, I asked them to spread in their class chats. The easiest was like, RVHS for eg. since they are already co-ed, so in addition to the guys I know can directly reach them, they are also more likely to feel effects of a highly imbalanced gender ratio and hence, more likely to join!

What I did for recruitment was ask people reach out directly (since I wasn't expecting that many people anyways) and then have 1-on-1 meeting to understand each of the interested people better. It was supposed to be more of a conversation than an interview, and honestly, I was probably significantly more scared than any of them were, and I had 0 clue what I was doing. Mainly, I was just trying to figure out: why she wants to join, what her experience in computing was like, any event-planning / teaching / publicity experience, and any suggestions she would have after I propose what I have already came up with. (Side note: I still remember, getting a notification from a girl reaching out and introducing herself saying she was from the previous year's SG national team for a computing-related thing, I was so happy but also so scared and panicked, because I felt that wasn't qualified to lead a team with someone like her inside.) Yeah, so from there, I formed a team of ~7 girls, mostly J1, from various JCs across Singapore.

Looking back on this part, I don't regret much. I think having that initial one-on-one talk was really helpful since people may be more hesitant to talk in group settings, but in that one-to-one setting, there isn't much choice but to talk. I think I still did it in too much of an interview style, and the amount that I shared about myself was solely dependent on my mood that day, so it wasn't really standardised, and so, some members may not know much about me, my computing experience and my journey as much as others, or as much as they should. Saying they 'should' because I think knowing enough about someone is crucial to see them as sort of a person instead of just an existing entity. Lastly, at this stage, I was kind of not embracing my story of being that only girl in my cohort taking computing, not sure why I was hesitant on sharing that detail. But I think that singular sentence would have been better at explaining the rationale behind me starting the project than all those statistics and yappery I used when people were trying to figure why I really started this.

Later on, I requested our sponsor, CSIT (see below), for permission to send a recruitment message to a Discord channel for a competition they were hosting. Although initially promising, this did not work out, since I think the demographic there was not the right fit as the competition was heavily marketed to complete beginners, and I would already know the more experiencedparticipants. (To be clear, I did not reject anyone, it was more like they found out more about us and then rejected us.)

## Collabs

In parallel while looking for girls to join my team, I was cold-messaging people on LinkedIn and emailing sponsorship/collaboration emails on company websites. This, did NOT work. On this part, I was not shameless enough, and sent less than 10 messages/emails total, which, looking back, obviously was not going to work.

During our first meeting, someone suggested Jane Street, since they were known to have sponsored girls' programmes. I did, and I remember being so excited that they agreed to sponsor us after only a few email exchanges. Granted, the amount we were asking for was merely a grain of salt and not remotely comparable to other events, but yeah, it really gave me hope for the future of the project. In addition to financial support, they also kindly sent some swag, which included T-shirts, notebooks, and pens. :0

An issue I faced here was that due to firm policies, they could only transfer to organisational bank accounts. I had no clue how this worked (and how money worked in general; I didn't even have a personal bank account at this point), and after some research and questioning several people, I concluded that given my circumstances (short on time and lack of official registration as any sort of organisation), the best option would be Hack Club Bank, and that's what I did. However, if you choose to do the same thing, just note that it's based in the US so money transfers and stuff are slightly more complicated than you may think, and of course, the main concern is that they take 7% of your money. Yeah...

The Centre for Strategic Infocomm Technologies (CSIT) was a sponsor at a competition I participated in. Admittedly, I initially approached them solely for the reason that a friend of mine did a research project with them, and I found it was particularly interesting and would have loved to do something similar. I don't remember what changed, but a week later, I asked the same friend for a contact (of the same CSIT representative I had spoken to) and they found our initiative unique from what they have seen, and agreed to sponsor us, as well as provide some venue liaising assistance (more on this later).

Frankly, I had never done this sort of outreach or dealing with sponsorships beforehand, and I'm sure they found my communications and methods vastly different from what they were used to (in a bad way). Other, more experienced and larger organisations offered things like tiered sponsorships, and drew up contracts and such, which I didn't see the need to figure out at that point in time.

Lastly, much later on, after signups was almost closing, someone suggested getting Cordy to do some promotion for us. They have quite a large reach via their Telegram bot and other means, and I'm glad they have decided to support our cause.

## 1st major issue: venue

This was the first major roadblock, and a headache for me for SO FREAKING LONG. Believe me when I say I tried SO MANY things.

1. Volunteers ask their schools. Unfortunately there is only a small number of us from each school, so as an organisation we don't 'belong' to any school, so there is not really an incentive for any school to help us either.
2. My own school. Once again, I'm the only one from my school. They would need a long timeline and they need to justify supporting us and not the 50 million other projects students pitch, which is difficult. They also talked about passing the project down to a junior, which I was not willing to promise.
3. NUS WiT and NUS Hackers. Issue: they are also students and there really isn't a good way for them to justify to their teachers that they should book a venue for us when we don't even need them to be so involved. Also, our event dates are holidays for them, so their teacher advisers can't be around either, and like, we're kids.
4. Got ghosted by SMU (which CSIT reached out for us) and SUTD.

Note that each of these took about 2 weeks on their own because I couldn't go through all that trouble just to tell a JC/university that someone else has already agreed, now could I?

In the final venue-hunting weeks, while CSIT was liaising with NUS for us, we were also hunting for external venues to book, which CSIT would then sponsor the money for. The external venues take so long to respond, and usually they don't fulfill the requirements we want, which is just a basic classroom, but why would there be classrooms outside of schools? I also pulled in some connections and I had 2 venues ready but then CSIT was in the process of confirming with NUS, and I was ghosting the venue contacts for a while. The timings for our workshops are also kind of funny because we prefer afternoon but those slots were booked on some days.

## 2nd major issue: participant sign-ups

You can't have an event without sign-ups, we were struggling so much for several reasons:

1. **Sign-ups were open late** since I was afraid to open them without confirming a venue first, which was extremely delayed. I suppose some people already had plans for the holidays by then.
2. **Schools do not respond to my emails.** In their defence, they were also sent late because of (1), and it is difficult for schools to promote nearing the end of a school term since they're busy handling admin themselves, and sending emails so close to holidays is weird by itself, I guess. Now, in MY defence, I emailed nearly 40 schools, and in total, received 3 replies: 1 saying yes, 2 saying no due to above reasons. Yup.
3. **I am not in the circles of my target audience.** We were targeting girls without any experience in coding, and maybe interested in STEM/tech in general. My school has a compulsory CS programme in Sec 1, and I'm slightly a nerd, so hmm.
4. **We don't have an Instagram** to try to blow up because I didn't believe in Instagram and the effort it took to maintain it. Not sure how I feel about this, like, I don't think we could have blown up and reached our target audience effectively this way either(?) (We have one now! Follow us @eos_initiative!)

Other than becoming more shameless and asking even more random people to spread the word from the most random discord channels, I also heard about this supposedly magical thing known as Meta Ads. It was working rather magically for this other organisation, and I'm extremely grateful to them, BuildingBlocs, for setting up Ads for us under their account. Unfortunately, and I'm still not sure why, this did not work well for us. A few friends of mine -- nerdy, CS, MEN -- saw the Ad, and informed me, which is completely the opposite of my target audience. Not sure why.

There isn't much of a conclusion here. If you're here for advise on anything you may be working on, just try everything. I don't know when Meta Ads would be useful, when it will not be; I don't know whether managing an Instagram account is worth it or how easy it would be to blow up; I don't know whether you should choose to approach organisations to broadcast for you. Yeah, I don't got a conclusion from my experiences :<

## Things im proud of

1. **Bonding dinners!**  
	We've haven't even had that many, but I'm so, so glad for each and every one that I've organised. I think that each of the members have their own interesting journey in computing, and really, life in general, and on top of that, each school is really unique and I enjoy finding out about systems outside of my school's 'bubble'. Also, also spending so much time around guys this year, I really enjoy the company of other girls with the same interests as me, and I think knowing each other better makes working together so much easier as well. I hope everyone enjoyed them as well :DD
2. **Outreach**  
	Coming from a place without any experience on this front, I'm really proud of the outcomes. TLDR: be VERY shameless. No one really gives a shit about you or what you're trying to do, so it's in your best interest to blast everywhere to hope someone somewhere cares enough to support you and gives you that chance. Research is important, yes, don't just blast that randomly, be strategic and place reasonable bets, but again, the best way to increase chances is just to take more of them. Also, connections is everything, exploit and pull on everything you can.
3. **Becoming an extrovert??**  
	Ok, maybe not, I still enjoy being in my own company sometimes, but I've really learned the importance of presenting yourself well to others, and just yapping, and getting to know others. Impressions matter so much more than I had thought, and in my case, computing really is such a small world so getting people to like you can be key to solving many problems.
4. **Lowkey... just existing**  
	It was just in my head less than a year ago, and now I have a team?? and I'm running a programme for actual students?? and sponsors?? and people know me as that girl who was recruiting for that girls in cs thing?? :p
